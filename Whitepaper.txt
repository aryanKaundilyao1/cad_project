1. Prediction Target Definition
Before any scoring metric can be justified, the engine must state — in falsifiable terms — what it is trying to predict. Every subsequent formula in this document exists to serve this definition.
1.1 Why a prediction target must be defined first
[PROVEN]  In supervised learning and decision science, a scoring function is only meaningful relative to a labeled outcome it is trained or evaluated against (Domingos, "A Few Useful Things to Know About Machine Learning", 2012). A score with no defined target is not a prediction — it is an opinion encoded as a number.

1.3 Recommended target definition
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  Define the primary label Y as a binary outcome: Y = 1 if a qualified meeting is booked within 30 days of first outreach touch, else Y = 0. Use "quote requested" and "deal closed" as secondary labels for a later multi-stage model (Section 9).
Rationale: "meeting booked" is (a) frequent enough to accumulate a usable sample size faster than closed deals, (b) unambiguous to log (binary event with a timestamp), and (c) a reasonable proxy for buying intent in B2B sales-cycles that often run 30–180+ days. This mirrors the standard RevOps practice of using Marketing/Sales-Qualified-Lead conversion as an intermediate label when final revenue outcomes are too sparse for early-stage modelling.
[HYPOTHESIS]  That "meeting booked within 30 days" is sufficiently correlated with eventual deal closure to be a useful proxy label for THIS specific business (industrial B2B, long cycles, relationship-driven). This must be tested once enough closed-deal data exists — see Section 6.4.
1.4 Formal specification
Let:   i = an opportunity (a company/contact/RFQ record)   X_i = feature vector for opportunity i (see Section 2)   Y_i ∈ {0,1} = qualified-meeting-booked-within-30-days indicator   f(X_i) = score assigned by the engine, f: X → [0,1] or [0,100]  Goal: f(X_i) should be monotonically associated with P(Y_i = 1 | X_i) i.e. higher-scored opportunities should convert at a higher observed rate than lower-scored opportunities, when measured historically.
[PROVEN]  This is the standard formal definition of a 'ranking model' calibration goal in information retrieval and lead-scoring literature — the score need not equal the true probability, but must preserve rank-order of true conversion likelihood (this is precisely what tools like AUC-ROC and lift/gain charts measure — Section 6).
1.5 What the target definition rules out
	•	The score is NOT a prediction of company size, financial health, or general "quality" — those are inputs, not the target.
	•	The score is NOT a probability in the strict Bayesian sense until calibrated against real outcomes (Section 8). Until then, it is only a ranking, not a probability.
	•	The score does NOT claim causal knowledge of why a company will buy — only correlational association between observable signals and past conversion, once data exists.

2. Mathematical Definition of Scoring Metrics
Each metric below follows the required structure: Definition → Required inputs → Mathematical formulation → Required data → Validation method → Limitations.
2.1 Composite Opportunity Score (COS) — top-level structure
[INDUSTRY BEST PRACTICE]  Weighted linear combination of sub-scores is the dominant architecture in commercial lead-scoring platforms (Salesforce Einstein, HubSpot, 6sense — see Section 12 benchmark) prior to those platforms layering in ML. It is chosen for interpretability, not because it is statistically optimal.
COS_i = Σ (w_k · S_k,i)   for k = 1..K sub-scores  subject to: Σ w_k = 1,  0 ≤ w_k ≤ 1,  0 ≤ S_k,i ≤ 100  where:   S_k,i = normalized score of sub-metric k for opportunity i   w_k   = weight of sub-metric k
	•	Required inputs: a set of normalized sub-scores S_k (Sections 2.2–2.7) and weights w_k (Section 4).
	•	Required data: none beyond what each sub-score independently requires.
	•	Validation method: Section 6 — rank-correlation of COS against realized Y outcomes (Spearman's ρ, AUC, lift).
[HYPOTHESIS]  That a LINEAR combination is adequate. Linear models assume sub-scores contribute independently and additively. If interactions exist (e.g. 'high budget signal' only matters when 'active tender' is also true), a linear model will systematically underperform an interaction-aware model such as a decision tree or gradient-boosted model (Section 13). This cannot be confirmed or refuted without outcome data.
2.2 Firmographic Fit Score (S_fit)
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  Measures how closely a company's observable attributes match the client's stated Ideal Customer Profile (ICP).
Definition: degree of match between opportunity attributes (industry code, employee count band, revenue band, geography, import/export activity) and client-defined ICP criteria.
S_fit,i = (1/N) · Σ_j  match(attr_j,i, ICP_j) · imp_j  where:   attr_j,i = attribute j of opportunity i (e.g. NAICS/HS code, geography)   ICP_j    = client-specified acceptable range/value for attribute j   match()  = 1 if within range, 0 if not (or partial match, see limitation)   imp_j    = client-assigned importance of attribute j (elicited, not invented)   N        = normalizing constant so S_fit ∈ [0,100]
	•	Required inputs: client ICP definition (elicited directly from client during onboarding, NOT invented by the engine).
	•	Required data: firmographic attributes per opportunity — industry classification, employee/revenue band, geography, trade-activity flags.
	•	Validation method: correlate S_fit with realized Y within a single client's outcome history once ≥30–50 outcomes exist (see Section 6.2 for minimum sample size justification).
	•	Limitations: binary match() is a simplification — a company "200 employees" vs an ICP of "50–500" is a full match, but distance-to-boundary information is discarded. A continuous distance-based function would be more information-preserving but requires calibration data to avoid arbitrary curve shapes.
2.3 Intent / Trigger-Event Score (S_intent)
[INDUSTRY BEST PRACTICE]  "Trigger event" or "intent signal" scoring is standard in commercial sales intelligence (Gartner's 2023–2024 Sales Intelligence Market Guide identifies intent data as a primary differentiator among vendors like 6sense, ZoomInfo, Bombora).
Definition: strength of evidence that a company has an active, time-bound need (e.g. published tender, expansion announcement, hiring surge in relevant function, active RFQ).
S_intent,i = max_m ( strength_m · recency_decay(t_m) )   over all detected trigger events m  recency_decay(t) = exp(−λ · t)     [PROVEN functional form — exponential decay]  where:   strength_m = base severity of trigger type m (e.g. active tender > hiring post)   t          = days since the event was observed   λ          = decay rate constant
[PROVEN]  Exponential decay is the standard, well-justified functional form for "information value decays over time" in survival analysis and information-retrieval recency weighting — the FORM of the curve is defensible.
[HYPOTHESIS]  The specific value of λ (how fast a signal decays) and the specific strength_m values per trigger type. Requires empirical validation using historical outcome data — specifically, survival-analysis of time-to-conversion conditioned on trigger type, once sufficient closed/lost outcomes exist.
	•	Required inputs: catalog of trigger-event types relevant to the client's industry (built manually per vertical, e.g. "Ashwagandha export ban" is irrelevant to PEB, "factory groundbreaking" is irrelevant to SaaS).
	•	Required data: timestamped detection of trigger events per opportunity, sourced per the Evidence Hierarchy (Section 3).
	•	Validation: time-to-event / survival analysis once outcome data exists; A/B comparison of scored vs unscored outreach prioritization.
	•	Limitations: multiple simultaneous trigger events are currently handled by max() (strongest signal wins) rather than combined — an untested simplification. Combining them (e.g. via a log-sum or additive model) requires justification that would itself need outcome data.
2.4 Data Reliability / Evidence Confidence Score (S_conf)
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  Distinct from all other sub-scores: this measures confidence IN the data, not desirability of the opportunity. It functions as a multiplicative gate, not an additive contributor — see Section 5.
S_conf,i = (1/M) · Σ_s  reliability(s) · verification_bonus(s)  where:   s = each evidence source contributing to opportunity i   reliability(s) = source-level reliability rating (Section 3 table)   verification_bonus(s) = additional weight if independently cross-verified   M = number of contributing sources
	•	Required inputs: source-reliability ratings (Section 3), cross-source verification logic.
	•	Required data: provenance metadata per data point — which source, when collected, whether cross-verified.
	•	Validation: compare false-positive rate (opportunities that turned out to be inaccurate/stale) across confidence bands, once outcome + data-quality audit logs exist.
[HYPOTHESIS]  That reliability ratings assigned in Section 3 (currently based on source-type reasoning, not measured error rates) reflect actual empirical accuracy. Requires a manual audit sample (e.g. randomly sampling 50–100 records per source and verifying against ground truth) before these ratings can be upgraded from heuristic to proven.
2.5 Engagement / Reachability Score (S_reach)
Definition: likelihood that a valid, responsive point of contact exists (verified email/phone, correct decision-maker title, active LinkedIn profile, etc.) — a pure data-quality gate on execution feasibility, independent of buying intent.
S_reach,i = w_1·verified_contact + w_2·title_match + w_3·channel_availability  (weights w_1..w_3: EXPERT HEURISTIC starting point — e.g. equal weighting 1/3 each —  Requires empirical validation using historical outcome data)
	•	Required inputs: contact verification status, title-to-decision-maker mapping (per Dream100 ABS templates already in use), available outreach channels.
	•	Required data: contact record completeness and verification timestamps.
	•	Validation: correlate with actual reply rate (a leading indicator available almost immediately, unlike Y itself) — this is the fastest sub-score to validate because feedback arrives in days, not months.
	•	Limitations: verified today ≠ reachable tomorrow — contact data decays (job changes, churn). No current decay model exists for this sub-score specifically (distinct from S_intent's decay, which models event relevance, not contact staleness).
2.6 Regulatory / Feasibility Risk Score (S_risk)
Definition: a DOWNWARD adjustment for opportunities carrying material feasibility risk that outreach effort alone cannot solve — e.g. a product-category import ban in the target market (Ashwagandha in Denmark/Sweden, per client-specific research already on file), sanctions exposure, or licensing gaps.
S_risk,i = 100 − Σ_r  penalty(r)   [floored at 0]  where r ranges over identified risk flags applicable to opportunity i
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  Penalty values per risk flag are not derivable from first principles — they require either (a) legal/regulatory expert judgment calibrated per market, or (b) empirical evidence of how often flagged opportunities failed to convert for that reason specifically.
	•	Required inputs: a maintained, industry-specific risk-flag registry (e.g. product-specific import restrictions, sanctions lists, licensing requirements) — this must be manually curated per vertical, not automatically inferred.
	•	Required data: regulatory status per product/market pair, refreshed on a defined cadence given regulations change (e.g. EU novel-food and herbal-supplement rulings).
	•	Validation: compare conversion rates of flagged vs unflagged opportunities within the same ICP band, once sufficient sample exists.
	•	Limitations: regulatory status can change faster than manual curation cycles — stale risk flags are a real failure mode; a refresh SLA must be defined and monitored.

3.2 Why source diversity is structurally required, not optional
[PROVEN]  In measurement theory, a scoring variable with a single, non-independent data source cannot produce informative variance if that source itself has low discriminative power — this is a direct restatement of the documented root-cause finding from the original COIF/OIE engine (all leads from Google Maps → no ability to differentiate priorities).
This is also consistent with the general principle of "feature diversity" in machine learning: models trained on collinear, single-origin features tend to overfit to that source's idiosyncrasies (e.g. Google Maps' business-category taxonomy) rather than learning transferable signal.
3.3 Cross-verification logic
verification_bonus(opportunity_i) =    HIGH  if attribute confirmed by ≥2 independent source TYPES   MED   if confirmed by 1 primary + 1 secondary source   LOW   if single-source only
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  The 2-independent-sources threshold is a common practice in OSINT (open-source intelligence) methodology and journalistic fact-checking ('two-source rule'), adapted here — not a statistically derived optimum for THIS engine specifically.

4. Signal Weighting Methodology
This section is the most important honesty checkpoint in the document: it must resist the temptation to assign confident numeric weights before data exists.
4.1 The core problem
[PROVEN]  In statistical learning theory, optimal weights for a linear (or any) scoring function are, by definition, those that minimize prediction error against a labeled outcome (e.g. via ordinary least squares, logistic regression, or a loss-minimizing algorithm). Without labeled outcomes (Y from Section 1), there is no mathematical procedure that can derive 'correct' weights — only priors.
Any document claiming to hand you a specific set of weights (e.g. "S_intent = 35%, S_fit = 25%...") without outcome data is presenting a guess as a fact. This report deliberately does not do that.

4.3 Formal weight-learning procedure once outcome data exists
[PROVEN]  Once ≥30–50 labeled outcomes exist (Section 6.2 justifies this threshold), weights can be estimated via logistic regression, where the sub-scores S_k are regressed against the binary outcome Y:
logit(P(Y=1 | S_1...S_K)) = β_0 + β_1·S_1 + β_2·S_2 + ... + β_K·S_K  The fitted coefficients β_k (rescaled to sum to 1 across positive coefficients) become the empirically-derived weights w_k, replacing the heuristic priors.
	•	Required data: labeled historical outcomes (Y) paired with the sub-scores that were in effect at the time of scoring (not retroactively recomputed — see Section 6.5 on avoiding leakage).
	•	Validation method: out-of-sample testing (train/test split or cross-validation) — PROVEN standard practice to avoid overfitting weights to noise, especially critical with small sample sizes.
	•	Limitations: with small samples (30–100 outcomes), coefficient estimates will have wide confidence intervals; report those intervals, do not treat point estimates as precise truths. Regularization (e.g. ridge/L2 penalty) is PROVEN to stabilize small-sample coefficient estimates and is recommended over unregularized regression at this stage.
4.4 Weight governance rules
	•	Every weight in the live system must carry a version tag and the date/sample-size it was last (re)estimated.
	•	Weights derived from <30 outcomes must be flagged in any client-facing output as "preliminary / low-confidence."
	•	No weight may be silently hand-tuned by a human 'because it feels right' without logging that this reverts the weight to EXPERT HEURISTIC status — this prevents heuristic drift from masquerading as validated output.

5.2 Full aggregation formula

[HYPOTHESIS]  Whether the gate should be linear, quadratic, or a step function (e.g. hard cutoff below a confidence threshold) cannot be determined without testing which gate shape best separates real converters from non-converters in historical data. Requires empirical validation using historical outcome data.
5.3 Why gating (not pure additive) is used for confidence and reachability
[PROVEN]  This mirrors a general principle in decision theory: a high expected-value estimate built on low-confidence information should be discounted proportionally to that uncertainty (related to the concept of 'expected utility under uncertainty', von Neumann–Morgenstern). The FORM of this principle is proven; the specific gate function is not.

6. Statistical Validation Methodology
This is the section that operationalizes "how do we know the engine works." It must be built BEFORE the engine is trusted, not after.
6.1 Core validation questions
	•	Discrimination: do higher-scored opportunities actually convert more often than lower-scored ones? (Rank quality)
	•	Calibration: does a score of 80 actually correspond to roughly an 80% conversion likelihood, or just 'better than 40'? (Section 8 covers this in depth)
	•	Stability: does the engine's ranking stay consistent across time periods and industries, or does it overfit to one client's pattern?
6.2 Minimum sample size before ANY validation claim is made
[PROVEN]  For a binary outcome with a rough conversion rate p, the standard rule-of-thumb for minimally stable logistic regression estimation is at least 10 events-per-variable (EPV) (Peduzzi et al., 1996, 'A simulation study of the number of events per variable in logistic regression analysis', Journal of Clinical Epidemiology — a widely cited methodological benchmark).
Minimum outcomes needed ≈ 10 × (number of predictors in the model)  Example: 5 sub-scores as predictors → ≈50 positive outcomes (Y=1 events) needed before weight-fitting (Section 4.3) should be trusted over the heuristic prior.
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  Below this threshold, JAS CONNECT should still LOG outcomes (essential for eventually reaching the threshold) but must not present fitted weights as validated — continue using heuristic priors and clearly label them as such.

6.5 Avoiding data leakage — a critical methodological trap
[PROVEN]  A well-documented failure mode in applied ML is training/validating a model using features that were not actually available at prediction time (e.g. using a company's eventual deal size to help predict whether they'd convert) — this inflates apparent accuracy in a way that will not replicate in production (Kaufman et al., 2012, 'Leakage in Data Mining').
	•	Implementation requirement: log the score AND the sub-score values AS THEY WERE AT THE TIME OF SCORING, not recomputed retroactively with information learned afterward.
	•	This requires a simple but essential piece of infrastructure: a scoring snapshot table, timestamped, immutable once written.
6.6 Cross-industry stability check
[HYPOTHESIS]  That a single scoring architecture generalizes across Ayurvedic export, PEB, and other verticals with only sub-score INPUT differences (not structural differences). This should be tested once outcome data exists in ≥2 distinct verticals, by checking whether weights fitted on one vertical transfer acceptably to another, or whether industry-specific re-weighting is required (see Section 10).

7. Commercial Validation Methodology
Statistical validity (Section 6) is necessary but not sufficient — the engine must also be shown to change commercial outcomes for a paying client, which is a distinct question.
7.1 Why statistical and commercial validation are different questions
[PROVEN]  A model can be statistically well-calibrated yet commercially useless if, for example, it correctly identifies good opportunities that a human sales rep would have found anyway with no effort — the relevant test is INCREMENTAL value over the counterfactual (existing manual process), not accuracy in isolation. This mirrors the standard 'uplift modelling' distinction in marketing science (Radcliffe & Surry, uplift modelling literature) — predicting response is not the same as predicting INCREMENTAL response caused by prioritization.
7.2 Recommended commercial test design
[INDUSTRY BEST PRACTICE]  A/B holdout test: split a comparable pool of opportunities into (A) scored & prioritized by the engine and (B) worked in the client's prior default order (e.g. chronological or alphabetical), holding outreach effort (touches, rep quality, script) constant across both groups.
Commercial lift = (Conversion rate_A − Conversion rate_B) / Conversion rate_B  Minimum sample size guidance for detecting a meaningful lift: use a standard two-proportion power calculation (PROVEN statistical method), not a fixed number — depends on baseline conversion rate and desired significance/power. A statistician or online sample-size calculator should be used per client/vertical rather than a single fixed number applied universally.
	•	Required data: two comparable opportunity pools, consistent outreach execution across both, tracked outcomes.
	•	Validation output: a confidence interval on the lift, not a single point estimate — small pilot sizes will have wide intervals; report them honestly.
	•	Limitations: true randomized holdouts are commercially uncomfortable for a client (withholding effort from some real opportunities) — a common compromise is a staggered rollout (score everything, but compare period-over-period before/after adoption), which is weaker evidence (confounded by seasonality, market conditions) but more palatable. This trade-off should be discussed explicitly with each client.

8. Calibration Methods
Calibration answers a narrower question than discrimination (Section 6.3): not "does the model rank well" but "does a score of X actually mean an X% chance of conversion." JAS CONNECT does not currently need probability-grade calibration to be USEFUL (ranking is enough for triage), but calibration becomes important if scores are ever shown to clients as implied probabilities.
8.1 Reliability diagrams / calibration curves
[PROVEN]  Standard method: bin predicted scores (e.g. into deciles), compute observed conversion rate within each bin, and plot predicted vs. observed. A perfectly calibrated model falls on the 45° line (Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, 'Predicting Good Probabilities With Supervised Learning', ICML 2005).
For each bin b:   predicted_b = mean(COS_i / 100) for i in bin b   observed_b  = mean(Y_i) for i in bin b  Calibration error = mean |predicted_b − observed_b| across bins (this is a simplified description of the Expected Calibration Error, ECE)
	•	Required data: sufficient volume per bin — with small N, bins will be noisy; this again depends on reaching the sample thresholds in Section 6.2.
	•	Validation: report calibration error alongside any client-facing probability claim; do not present a raw score as "X% chance" until this has been measured.

9.2 Why outcome logging is the single highest-priority infrastructure item
[PROVEN]  This is a direct restatement of a foundational principle in applied statistics and ML: a model cannot be validated, improved, or trusted without a labeled outcome dataset — no amount of formula sophistication substitutes for this (this matches the project's own standing principle that CRM/outcome logging is the prerequisite for everything).

	•	Every deployed scoring configuration (weights, gate functions, thresholds) must carry a version ID.
	•	Every scored opportunity snapshot must record which version scored it — required for honest backtesting and for detecting when a version underperforms.
	•	Rollback capability: if a new version underperforms the previous one on a validation batch, revert — this requires keeping prior versions live/comparable, not just archived.

11. Bias and Risk Analysis
A scoring engine that influences which real companies get contacted first carries real risk of systematic bias, not just statistical error. This section is required, not optional, for a commercially deployed prioritization system.
11.1 Sources of bias to actively monitor
Bias type
Mechanism
Mitigation
Source coverage bias
Sources like LinkedIn Sales Navigator, English-language directories, or Google Maps systematically under-represent certain geographies/company sizes/languages
Explicitly track source mix per opportunity pool; monitor whether certain segments are structurally invisible to the engine
Feedback / self-fulfilling prophecy bias
If only high-scored opportunities are ever contacted, the model can never learn whether low-scored ones would have converted — outcome data becomes conditioned on the model's own past decisions
Periodically contact a small random sample of low/medium-scored opportunities (an EXPLORATION set) purely to generate unbiased validation data — a direct application of the exploration/exploitation trade-off in decision theory
Survivorship bias in manual research
Analysts may unconsciously prioritize researching companies that 'look promising,' contaminating what counts as 'discovered' before scoring even happens
Standardize discovery criteria (Section 3) separately from scoring criteria; document discovery methodology per batch
Confirmation bias in weight tuning
A human noticing the model 'missed' a deal and manually bumping a weight up based on one anecdote
Governed weight-update process (Section 4.4) — no ad hoc single-anecdote weight changes
Regulatory/risk-flag staleness bias
Outdated risk flags (Section 2.6) can systematically and unfairly deprioritize opportunities in a category after the actual regulatory situation has changed
Defined refresh SLA per risk-flag registry, especially for fast-moving regulatory areas
[PROVEN]  The exploration/exploitation trade-off is a well-established concept in decision theory and reinforcement learning (multi-armed bandit literature, e.g. Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction) — always exploiting the current best-known score without any exploration systematically prevents the model from learning about regions of the input space it currently scores low.
11.2 Fairness considerations specific to B2B opportunity scoring
Unlike consumer credit or hiring scoring, B2B opportunity scoring does not typically raise protected-class fairness concerns in the same legal sense — but it can still create commercially unfair outcomes worth monitoring:
	•	Small/new companies may be systematically under-scored simply because they generate less public data (less press, thinner LinkedIn presence) — not because they are worse prospects.
	•	Non-English-market companies may be under-scored due to source language coverage gaps, not genuine lower opportunity quality.
[HYPOTHESIS]  Whether these under-scoring patterns are commercially significant enough to warrant a structural correction (e.g. a 'data-sparsity adjustment') is untested and should be examined once enough opportunity-pool composition data exists.

13.2 Why NOT to jump straight to Phase 4
[PROVEN]  This follows directly from the 'no free lunch' and parsimony principles in statistical learning: a more complex model (e.g. gradient boosting) requires substantially more data to estimate reliably than a linear model, and without that data will simply overfit — it will not magically outperform a simple model just because it is more sophisticated (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, is the standard reference for this trade-off).

Executive Verdict — Review Board Summary
Answering the original charge directly: is the JAS CONNECT scoring engine, as currently specified, scientifically valid, statistically defensible, commercially useful, and capable of predicting real buying opportunities?
Scientifically valid?
The ARCHITECTURE is scientifically sound — sub-score decomposition, evidence-weighted aggregation, and a defined prediction target are all standard, defensible design choices consistent with both academic decision-science literature and mature commercial platforms (Section 12). However, the current NUMERIC WEIGHTS are explicitly unvalidated priors, not scientific findings — this document does not, and should not, claim otherwise.
Statistically defensible?
Not yet, and cannot be, until historical outcome data exists (Section 6.2's sample-size thresholds are not currently met — zero historical outcome data was confirmed for this engine). The document specifies exactly what would make it statistically defensible (Sections 4.3, 6, 8) — this is a currently-unmet bar with a clear, evidence-based path to meeting it, not a permanent limitation.
Commercially useful?
[EXPERT HEURISTIC]  Even a heuristic-weighted, unvalidated rule-based score is likely more useful than no prioritization at all (working an unranked list, or a single-source list per the documented COIF/OIE failure) — PROVIDED it is used for triage/ranking only, and never presented to clients as a validated probability (Section 8.3). This is a reasonable operating assumption, not a proven commercial fact, and should itself be tested via the A/B or staggered-rollout methodology in Section 7.2.
Capable of predicting real buying opportunities?
Capable of RANKING opportunities by a defensible, multi-source, evidence-weighted heuristic today. Not yet capable of a scientifically validated PREDICTION in the strict statistical sense — that capability is earned through the phased roadmap in Section 13, gated on outcome data that does not yet exist.
Single most important recommendation
[PROVEN]  Every other recommendation in this document is downstream of one dependency: build outcome logging now. Nothing in Sections 4, 6, 7, 8, or 13 can progress past its current heuristic/hypothesis status without it. This is not a nice-to-have — it is the critical path.

Appendix B — Glossary of Statistical Terms Used
Term
Plain-language meaning
AUC-ROC
A single number (0.5-1.0) summarizing how well a score separates eventual converters from non-converters; 0.5 = random guessing, 1.0 = perfect separation
Calibration
Whether a score of 'X' actually corresponds to roughly an X% real-world conversion rate
Discrimination
Whether higher scores actually correspond to higher real conversion rates, regardless of exact probability accuracy
Events-per-variable (EPV)
A rule of thumb for how many positive outcomes you need before trusting a fitted statistical model with a given number of input variables
Logistic regression
A standard statistical method for estimating the relationship between input variables and a yes/no outcome
Overfitting
A model that fits noise in a small sample so closely that it fails to generalize to new, unseen data
Regularization (L2/ridge)
A mathematical technique that shrinks coefficient estimates toward zero to reduce overfitting, especially useful with small samples
Spearman rank correlation
A statistic measuring whether two variables move in the same relative order, without requiring a straight-line relationship
